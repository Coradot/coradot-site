The broker compliance and file review checklist for every transaction

A practical checklist a managing broker or compliance lead can run on every listing and purchase file, from the signed agreement to commission.

November 4, 2026

Brokers are responsible for the files their agents produce. State license laws generally hold the supervising broker accountable for reasonable supervision of agents and for keeping complete transaction records. A file review checklist turns that responsibility into a routine: the same questions, asked at the same points, on every file. Adapt the list below to your state's forms and your brokerage's policies.

When to review

Reviewing once, after closing, is an audit, not supervision. Most brokerages get the best results from four review points:

  • At the signed agreement, before the file moves forward.
  • After contingencies are resolved.
  • Before settlement, when the file should be complete.
  • Before commission is disbursed.

At each point the reviewer should be able to approve the file, return it to the agent with a specific note or put it on hold. Record which one, who did it and when.

Decide in advance who reviews what. A compliance lead or transaction coordinator can confirm that documents are present and signed, while the managing broker makes the calls that need judgment, such as an unusual addendum, a dual agency file or a commission exception. Writing that split down keeps reviews consistent across offices and teams.

Agreement and agency

  • The listing agreement or buyer agreement is fully signed and dated, on the current approved form.
  • Required agency or brokerage relationship disclosures were delivered when your state requires and acknowledged in writing.
  • Under the practice changes that took effect in August 2024, agents at REALTOR®-affiliated MLSs need a written agreement with a buyer before touring a home. Confirm it is on file and states the compensation terms.
  • Any dual agency or designated agency consent is signed by both clients.
  • The agent's license is active in the property's state.

Contract and addenda

  • The purchase agreement is fully executed, with the ratification date clear.
  • Every addendum the contract references is in the file and signed.
  • Every change after ratification was made by a signed amendment, not by editing an executed document.
  • Initials are present everywhere the form requires them.
  • Names, property address, price and settlement date match across the contract, the addenda and the lender and title documents.

Disclosures

  • The seller's property disclosure, or the exemption your state allows, signed by the seller and acknowledged by the buyer.
  • The lead-based paint disclosure for homes built before 1978, with the required pamphlet delivered.
  • Condo or HOA resale documents delivered, with the delivery date recorded, since delivery often starts a review period.
  • Any local disclosures your jurisdiction requires, such as flood zone, utilities or special assessments.

Earnest money

  • The deposit receipt, showing the amount, the holder and the date received.
  • Proof the deposit was placed in escrow or trust within the time your state requires.
  • Any release of deposit signed by both parties.
  • Wiring instructions confirmed by phone at a known number, never by a number in an email.

Deadlines and contingencies

  • Each contingency shows how it ended: satisfied, removed in writing, waived or used to terminate.
  • Inspection responses and repair agreements are signed.
  • Financing and appraisal outcomes are documented.
  • No open deadline remains on the file at settlement.

Settlement and commission

  • The final settlement statement is in the file, and the commission on it matches the agreements.
  • The commission disbursement authorization matches the settlement statement.
  • Referral and cooperating brokerage payments are documented.
  • The file is complete before commission is paid.

Make the checklist enforceable

A checklist on paper depends on someone remembering to use it. The more of it that lives in the file itself, the less depends on memory. Three practices help most:

  • Tie the checklist to the file's jurisdiction, transaction type and property type, so a condo purchase and a single-family listing each get the right items.
  • Limit agents to the brokerage's approved forms, in their current versions, so the first question on the checklist is answered before review starts.
  • Require broker approval at the points that matter, so a file can't move past a checkpoint until someone has looked at it.

Then keep the review trail. When a regulator or a client asks how a file was supervised, the answer should be in the record: who reviewed it, when, what they flagged and how it was resolved. The same record shows your agents exactly what a clean file looks like, which makes the next review faster.

Frequently asked questions

What should a broker review on every transaction file?

The signed agreement and agency disclosures, the executed contract and every addendum, required property disclosures, earnest money handling, how each contingency ended, and the settlement statement and commission before payment.

How often should a broker review transaction files?

At set checkpoints rather than only after closing: at the signed agreement, after contingencies, before settlement and before commission. Reviewing during the transaction catches problems while they can still be fixed.

How long does a brokerage need to keep transaction files?

Your state's license law sets the retention period, which commonly runs several years from closing. Keep the complete file, including executed documents, disclosures and the review trail, for the full period.
