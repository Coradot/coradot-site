Real estate transaction management for brokerages: from signed agreement to closing

A practical map of what a brokerage's transaction process should cover, step by step, from the signed agreement to the closed file.

November 4, 2026

Most brokerages don't lose deals for lack of software. They lose time, money and sometimes standing with their regulator because the transaction lives in too many places: a form library on a shared drive, signatures in one tool, deadlines in an agent's calendar, approvals in a broker's inbox. Transaction management is the discipline of pulling those pieces into one process that every file follows. Here is what that process should cover.

Start the file at the signed agreement

The transaction begins the moment a listing agreement or purchase agreement is signed, not when the file is first uploaded for review. Open the file at that point and capture the basics once: property address, parties, representation (listing side, buying side or both), price, the ratification or acceptance date and the settlement date. Everything after this depends on those facts being right, so the person who enters them should be the person holding the executed agreement.

Run one file per side of the deal. If your brokerage represents both buyer and seller, two files keep each side's obligations, documents and communications clean, and make the required dual agency or designated agency consents easier to check.

Use the forms your brokerage approved, in their current versions

Forms change. Associations and regulators publish new versions, and an agent who keeps an old PDF on a laptop will keep using it. A transaction process should offer agents only the forms the brokerage has approved for that market and property type, and retire superseded versions on a schedule the broker sets.

The checklist for each file should follow from three facts: the jurisdiction, whether it is a listing or a purchase, and the property type. A condo purchase in one county needs different documents from a single-family listing in another. Building the checklist from those facts, instead of one generic list, is how you stop documents from going missing in the first place.

Put deadlines on the file, not in someone's head

Once the contract is ratified, its dates start running: earnest money delivery, inspection, financing, appraisal, condo or HOA document review, title review and settlement. Calculate each one from the contract's own terms and the acceptance date, show it on the file where the agent, the transaction coordinator and the broker can all see it, and send reminders before it arrives. When a contingency is removed or an amendment moves a date, the file should change the same day. Our guide to tracking contract deadlines and contingencies goes deeper.

Sign where the documents live

Every signature request should start from the file and land back in the file. When agents send documents from a separate e-signature account, the executed copy ends up as an email attachment and the brokerage's file stays incomplete until someone remembers to upload it. Signatures tied to the transaction keep the form, the signers, the order they signed in and the completed copy together. See e-signatures and document packages for what to require.

Review at checkpoints, not only at the end

Broker review that happens only after closing finds problems after they have become expensive. Set review points where they matter: when the agreement is executed, when contingencies are resolved, before settlement and before commission is disbursed. At each point the reviewer should be able to approve the file, return it with a specific note or hold it, and every one of those actions should be recorded with who took it and when. Our broker compliance and file review checklist lists what to check at each point.

Keep every party working from the same facts

A transaction involves far more people than the two agents: buyers and sellers, the lender, the title or escrow company, sometimes a condo or homeowners association, inspectors and appraisers. Keep one directory of who they are, share the terms, key dates and contacts with the people who need them, and keep a log of what was sent and when. Fewer "just checking in" emails is the visible benefit; fewer missed handoffs is the real one. More in keeping agents, clients, lenders and title on the same page.

Close with a complete, locked record

When the transaction closes, the file should already be complete. Executed documents should be locked, with any later change made through a new document or an amendment rather than an edit. The record should show every approval, every signature and every action, each attributed to a person. Your state's license law sets how long you keep transaction records, and a complete, locked file is exactly what you want to hand an auditor or produce in a dispute years later.

What to look for in a transaction management platform

  • One file per side of the deal, opened from the signed agreement.
  • Checklists that follow jurisdiction, transaction type and property type.
  • Broker control over which forms and versions agents can use.
  • Deadlines calculated from the contract and visible to everyone on the file.
  • Signatures built in and tied to the transaction.
  • Review points where the broker can approve, return or hold a file.
  • Role-based access, so each person sees what their role allows.
  • An audit trail and a locked record at close.

If a platform covers only part of this list, the rest ends up back in email and spreadsheets, which is where the trouble started.

Frequently asked questions

What is transaction management in real estate?

It is the process a brokerage uses to run each deal from the signed agreement to closing: opening the file, using approved forms, tracking deadlines, collecting signatures, reviewing the file and keeping a complete record. Good transaction management puts all of that in one place every party can rely on.

When should a brokerage open a transaction file?

At the signed listing agreement or purchase agreement. Opening the file then, rather than when documents are first sent for review, means deadlines, forms and parties are tracked from the first day the agreement is in force.

Is transaction management software the same as e-signature software?

No. E-signature tools collect signatures. Transaction management covers the whole file, including forms, deadlines, parties, broker review and the final record, with signatures as one part of it.
