E-signatures and document packages for real estate: what a brokerage needs

What a brokerage should require from electronic signatures and document packages so every executed file is complete, provable and easy to review.

November 4, 2026

Electronic signatures are routine in residential real estate. Agreements, addenda, disclosures and amendments get signed on phones in minutes. But speed isn't the measure that matters to a broker. The measure is whether, months or years later, you can show exactly what was signed, by whom, in what order and on which version of the form, and whether the executed copy in your file is the one the parties actually signed.

The legal footing

In the United States, the federal ESIGN Act and state electronic transaction laws, most of them based on the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), give electronic signatures the same effect as handwritten ones for most contracts, including listing and purchase agreements. These laws rest on a few practical requirements: the signer intends to sign, agrees to do business electronically, the signature is tied to the record, and the record is kept in a form that can be accurately reproduced later.

Documents that must be notarized or recorded, such as a deed, follow their own rules set by the state, the county recorder and the settlement agent. Many states now permit remote online notarization, and the title or escrow company will tell you what it accepts for a given closing.

What to require from e-signatures

  • Signer identity: who signed, from which email address or phone, and when.
  • Consent: a record that each signer agreed to sign electronically.
  • Signing order: when one party must sign before another, the order is enforced.
  • Fields tied to the right party: buyer fields signed by the buyer and seller fields by the seller, with each co-buyer and co-seller signing their own.
  • A completion certificate: a summary of the signing events that shows the document hasn't changed since it was signed.
  • Filing: the executed copy saved to the transaction automatically, not left in an agent's inbox.

Start from the right form, in its current version

An e-signature is only as good as the document it's attached to. If an agent uploads an outdated purchase agreement and sends it out, you now have a perfectly executed contract on the wrong form. Start every signature request from the brokerage's approved form library, with superseded versions retired, so the form question is settled before anyone signs.

Lock what's executed

Once every party signs, the document should be locked. Any change after execution, from a corrected price to a new settlement date, should be made through a new signed amendment or addendum, not by editing the executed file. That way the record shows the original agreement and every change to it, in order, each signed by the parties.

Build document packages around the transaction

A document package is the set of forms that go out for signature together: a listing package, an offer, a disclosure set, an amendment. Good packages share a few traits:

  • They're assembled from the file's checklist, so the right forms for the jurisdiction, transaction type and property type are included.
  • Each form is routed to the people who actually sign it, rather than sending every page to everyone.
  • Details entered once, such as names, address and price, carry into each form instead of being retyped.
  • When the package is complete, each executed form lands in its place on the file and the checklist updates.

Keep the signing trail with the file

The signing trail, meaning who was sent what, when they signed and the completion certificate, should stay with the transaction for as long as your state requires you to keep the file. When a broker reviews the file, they should see the signing status of every document without logging into a separate account. Mistakes to avoid

  • Agents using personal e-signature accounts, so the brokerage never holds the executed copies.
  • Signing on outdated forms pulled from a desktop folder.
  • Editing an executed PDF instead of writing an amendment.
  • Sending the full contract to parties who only need to sign a disclosure.
  • Losing track of which co-buyer or co-seller still needs to sign.

Each of these turns a fast signature into a slow problem at review or settlement. Signing that runs through the transaction itself, as described in our guide to transaction management from signed agreement to closing, removes most of them at once.

Frequently asked questions

Are electronic signatures valid on real estate contracts?

Yes, for most documents. The federal ESIGN Act and state electronic transaction laws give e-signatures the same effect as handwritten signatures on listing and purchase agreements. Documents that must be notarized or recorded, such as a deed, follow their own rules.

What is an e-signature completion certificate?

A record produced when every party has signed, listing the signers and the time of each signing event, and showing that the document has not changed since it was signed. It belongs in the transaction file with the executed document.

How should changes be made after a contract is signed?

With a new signed amendment or addendum. The executed contract stays locked, and the record shows the original agreement and every change to it in order.
