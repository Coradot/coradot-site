How brokerages track contract deadlines and contingencies so nothing is missed

A missed deadline can cost a buyer their deposit or a seller their sale. Here is how to build a deadline process that holds on every file.

November 4, 2026

Almost every residential purchase contract is a schedule. From the moment it is ratified, dates start running: when the deposit is due, how long the buyer has to inspect, when financing must be approved, when condo documents must be delivered and reviewed, when the parties settle. Many contracts treat those dates as firm, and a contingency that isn't acted on in time can lapse, be treated as waived or give the other side the right to cancel, depending on how your state's contract is written. Brokerages that don't miss dates don't rely on memory. They run a process.

Know what starts the clock

Every deadline counts from something. In most contracts that is the date of ratification, acceptance or final signature and delivery, often called Day 0. Get that date right, in writing, on the file, before calculating anything else. If the contract counts calendar days, weekends count; if it counts business days, they don't, and the contract or state law will say which holidays are excluded. Note whether a deadline ends at a specific time of day.

Your state's contract and your brokerage's policies set the deadlines. The job of your process is to read them from the actual executed contract on each file, not from a template of typical timelines.

The deadlines most residential files carry

  • Earnest money deposit: when the deposit must be delivered and to whom, and when the holder must place it in escrow or trust under your state's rules.
  • Home inspection: the inspection period, the deadline to deliver a repair request and the seller's response window.
  • Lead-based paint: for homes built before 1978, federal rules give buyers a 10-day opportunity for a risk assessment or inspection unless the parties agree to a different period or the buyer waives it in writing.
  • Financing: when the buyer must apply, when the loan commitment is due and when the financing contingency expires.
  • Appraisal: the deadline for the appraisal and for any response if it comes in below the contract price.
  • Condo or HOA documents: delivery of the resale package and the buyer's review or rescission period that follows it.
  • Title: delivery of the title commitment and the window to object to title issues.
  • Sale of the buyer's home: if the contract depends on it, along with any kick-out notice period.
  • Final walk-through and settlement.

Not every file has every item, and some have others, such as a well and septic inspection, a survey or a lease review on a tenant-occupied property. That is why each file's checklist should follow from its jurisdiction, transaction type and property type.

Calculate once, then recalculate on every change

Deadlines move. An amendment extends the inspection period, a contingency is removed early, settlement slips a week. The most common way a date gets missed is that it was correct on day one and stale by day twenty. Each time an amendment or addendum is executed, update the affected dates on the file that day and tell the people who rely on them. When a contingency is removed, mark it removed, record the document that removed it and stop the reminders. If the parties later agree to reopen it, reopen it the same way, on the record.

Make every date visible to everyone who acts on it

A deadline the agent knows about and the transaction coordinator doesn't is half-tracked. Show the dates on the file itself, where the agent, the coordinator and the broker see the same list. Clients should see the dates that ask something of them: the deposit, the inspection, the loan application, the walk-through. Lenders and title need the financing and settlement dates. One shared source beats five calendars kept in sync by hand.

Remind early and escalate before the deadline

Set reminders with enough lead time to act. A reminder on the day the inspection period ends is a notice of a missed opportunity. A practical pattern is a reminder when the period opens, another a few days before it closes and a final one the day before. Give the managing broker a daily view of what is due across all active files, so a file with a deadline approaching and no activity against it stands out without anyone opening every file.

Document how each contingency ended

When a file closes, the record should show how every contingency was resolved: satisfied, removed by a signed document, waived or used to terminate. That record protects the client, the agent and the brokerage if anyone later asks what happened and when. It also makes broker review faster, because the reviewer can see at a glance that nothing was left open. A deadline routine for transaction coordinators

  1. Confirm the ratification date and how the contract counts days.
  2. Enter every deadline from the executed contract and each addendum.
  3. Share the dates with the agent, the client, the lender and title.
  4. Set reminders ahead of each date, not on it.
  5. Update dates the same day any amendment is signed.
  6. Record how each contingency was resolved before the file goes to review.

Run the same routine on every file and the deadlines stop depending on who happens to be handling it. For the wider process around these dates, see transaction management from signed agreement to closing.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if a contingency deadline is missed?

It depends on how the contract is written. In some contracts the contingency is treated as waived; in others the other party gains the right to cancel. That is why each deadline should be tracked from the executed contract on the file rather than from a general timeline.

Should contract deadlines be counted in calendar days or business days?

Use whatever the contract says. Many residential contracts count calendar days unless they state otherwise, while others use business days or set a specific time of day. Confirm the definition before calculating any date.

Who should track deadlines at a brokerage?

The agent owns the deadlines on their file, a transaction coordinator often manages them day to day and the broker oversees them across all files. All three should be looking at the same dates in the same place.
